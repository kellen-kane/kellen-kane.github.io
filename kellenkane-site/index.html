I am a public policy scholar who studies how variation in subnational politics and policy shapes health and socioeconomic outcomes for historically marginalized communities, with particular attention to sexual and reproductive health and LGBTQ+ communities. I use econometrics, methods from applied geography, and text analysis. With Tracee Saunders and Candis Watts Smith, I am writing Unplanning Parenthood, a book on how partisan pendulum swings and policy precarity have eroded access to contraception through the federal Title X program.

At the LBJ School, I teach core and elective courses in public affairs, public management, and social policy, and I direct undergraduate student success and enrichment. Before joining UT, I was a Program Evaluator at the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, where I designed and led a multi-agency randomized controlled trial of a federal Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility training program and studied the uptake of the federal paid parental leave benefit and the effects of telework and remote work policies. I began that partnership as an OPM Academic Affiliate while a Research Fellow with the Better Government Lab at Georgetown's McCourt School of Public Policy.

I earned my Ph.D. in Public Policy from the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, where I hold a fixed-term graduate faculty appointment, and my MPA and B.S. in Cell and Molecular Biology from West Chester University of Pennsylvania.

Research

My research asks why policies meant to improve health and socioeconomic outcomes so often fail to reach the people who need them most. I trace this puzzle from the politics that shape which health and social policies get written and adopted, through the administrative variation that determines whether they are implemented as designed, to the uneven health and economic consequences that follow, even for policies with broad consensus or explicit targeting.

My work appears in Health Economics, Policy and Law, Health Affairs Scholar, Politics & Gender, the Journal of Health Politics, Policy and Law, and The Forum, and has been covered by The Economist, Forbes, and PBS and cited in a proposed rule by the U.S. Department of the Treasury. It has been recognized with APSA's Kenneth Sherrill Prize, the State Politics and Policy Conference's Thomas Carsey Scholar Award, and an award from the Society for Family Planning, and supported by the Williams Institute at UCLA School of Law.

Unplanning Parenthood: The Politics of Partisan Pendulum Swings, Policy Precarity, and Contraception Deserts

Kellen A. Kane, Tracee Saunders, and Candis Watts Smith

Americans disagree on many things, but on one issue their consensus is striking: people should be able to access birth control. President Nixon signed Title X, the only federal program dedicated to family planning, with bipartisan support in 1970, and roughly three-quarters of Americans, including majorities across party lines, support protecting access to contraception today. Yet in the post-Dobbs era, contraception access has grown more contested, and the places where people lack it, which we call contraception deserts, are growing.

Unplanning Parenthood examines this paradox: a policy domain with near-universal public support that has nonetheless been eroded through bureaucratic rule changes, intergovernmental conflict, and partisan polarization. We argue that partisan pendulum swings at the national level, the nationalization of political parties, and high-stakes power struggles across levels of government together produce policy precarity, destabilizing the reproductive health care that Title X historically provided to the communities with the greatest need. The book focuses on the Trump-Biden-Trump years, when this whiplash has been most aggressive.

We pair a census-tract-level atlas of contraception deserts across all 50 states and DC with interviews with the Title X administrators who allocate the program's resources, and we use multilevel regression and poststratification to link access to health outcomes.

The Changing Landscape of PrEP Deserts in the United States

Kellen A. Kane

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a critical intervention that greatly reduces the risk of HIV transmission, yet limited access to prescribing providers continues to constrain uptake in the United States. This study examines how geographic access to PrEP providers changed between 2020 and 2023 and identifies areas with limited access, referred to as PrEP deserts. Using the Integrated two-step floating catchment area method, this study finds that despite widespread gains in PrEP access, five states experienced increases in the total population living in PrEP deserts. Additionally, more than 5 million residents in states designated as priority jurisdictions under the Ending the HIV epidemic initiative gained geographic access to PrEP providers between 2020 and 2023. However, improvements were uneven: in some states, communities of colour experienced declining access and remain disproportionately represented in PrEP deserts. These findings highlight persistent geographic and demographic disparities in HIV prevention infrastructure despite recent federal efforts to expand PrEP availability.

Implementing Title X: Policy Design, Administration, and Geographic Access to Care

Tracee M. Saunders, Kellen A. Kane, and Candis Watts Smith

Title X of the Public Health Service Act is a critical federal program—the only one dedicated exclusively to providing affordable, confidential, evidence-based reproductive health care to low-income populations, yet its capacity to fulfill this mandate depends on political, administrative, and fiscal decisions made across multiple levels of governance—from Congress and federal regulators to state and regional grantees and their sub-grantee clinic networks. This study assesses geographic access to Title X-funded clinics across all 50 states and the District of Columbia in 2024, a period with significant program and regulatory disruption. Using the Integrated Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (I2SFCA) method—which employs road-network-based drive time thresholds calibrated to urban–rural context—applied to 2024 clinic locations and census-tract-level demographic data, we identify a confluence of spatial and nonspatial barriers to Title X. Findings reveal widespread and substantial gaps in geographic access, a challenge faced by people across all racial groups, rural and urban residents, and individuals with low incomes. These findings offer a precise geographic account of where Title X's reach falls shortest—documenting the ramifications of policy retrenchment and establishing a baseline against which future policy changes can be measured.

Contraception Deserts: The Effects of Title X Rule Changes on Access to Reproductive Health Care Resources

Candis Watts Smith, Rebecca J. Kreitzer, Kellen A. Kane, and Tracee M. Saunders

Historically, access to contraception has been supported in a bipartisan way, best exemplified by consistent congressional funding of Title X—the only federal program specifically focused on providing affordable reproductive health care to American residents. However, in an era of partisan polarization, Title X has become a political and symbolic pawn, in part because of its connection to family planning organizations like Planned Parenthood. The conflicts around Title X highlight the effects of intertwining abortion politics and contraception policy, particularly as they relate to reproductive justice and gendered policy making. Family planning organizations like Planned Parenthood have responded to these battles by bowing out of the Title X network. To what extent have contraception deserts—places characterized by inequitable access to Title X—developed or expanded in response to policy changes related to contraception and reproductive health? What is the demographic makeup of these spaces of inequality? We leverage data from the Office of Population Affairs and the U.S. Census Bureau and use the integrated two-step floating catchment area method to illustrate the effects of a major change in the Title X network in 10 states. Our results reveal the widespread human ramifications of increasing constraints on family planning organizations as a result of quiet but insidious federal bureaucratic rule changes.

Affordable but Inaccessible? Contraception Deserts in the US States

Rebecca J. Kreitzer, Candis Watts Smith, Kellen A. Kane, and Tracee M. Saunders

This article focuses on whether, and the extent to which, the resources made available by Title X—the only federal policy aimed specifically at reproductive health care—are equitably accessible. Here, equitable means that barriers to accessing services are lowest for those people who need them most. The authors use geographic information systems (GIS) and statistical/spatial analysis (specifically the integrated two-step floating catchment area [I2SFCA] method) to study the spatial and nonspatial accessibility of Title X clinics in 2018. The authors find that contraception deserts vary across the states, with between 17% and 53% of the state population living in a desert. Furthermore, they find that low-income people and people of color are more likely to live in certain types of contraception deserts. The analyses reveal not only a wide range of sizes and shapes of contraception deserts across the US states but also a range of severity of inequity.

The journal's second most-read article of 2021. Covered by The Economist, Forbes, and PBS and cited in a proposed rule by the U.S. Department of the Treasury.

The Evolution of Morality Policy Debate: Moralization and Demoralization

Rebecca J. Kreitzer, Kellen A. Kane, and Christopher Z. Mooney

Scholars of morality policy have built an extensive literature surrounding these issues, which often are associated with unusual political behavior. Early studies aimed at explaining this behavior but avoided defining a “morality policy” explicitly, typically by focusing on issues that appeared obviously to pertain to morality, like abortion and LGBTQ+ rights. Drawing on the existing morality policy scholarship and classic theories of public policy, we argue that no public policy is inherently moral. Rather, policies may be “moralized” or “demoralized” over time, not due to any intrinsic characteristic, but because the prominent policy frames in their debate have changed. Public opinion and its proxies, along with certain exogenous shocks, may be important in determining when a morality frame will be more prevalent. Because the distinctiveness of morality policy lies in the discourse surrounding it, scholars should examine the behavior and attitudes of relevant advocates in these debates, rather than relying on aggregate data and making assumptions about intrinsic policy characteristics.

The Effect of Conversion Therapy Bans on Youth Mental Health and Suicidality

Kellen A. Kane and Brennan Mange

Conversion therapy, a widely discredited practice that attempts to change a person's sexual orientation or gender identity, remains legal for minors in many states. Using survey data from the Youth Risk Behavior Survey and mortality data from the National Vital Statistics System, we estimate the effect of state bans on conversion therapy for minors on LGBTQ+ youth mental health and suicidality. We find that conversion therapy bans decrease the suicide rate among middle- and late-adolescent individuals, particularly among males.

Credits Where They Are Due? Health Effects of Expanded State EITCs for LGBTQ+ Individuals

Kellen A. Kane

The Earned Income Tax Credit is frequently held up as one of the most effective anti-poverty tools in social policy. Exploiting variation in the timing and generosity of state EITC supplements, I assess its health effects for individuals in same-sex households. While EITCs show no aggregate health effect for same-sex households relative to different-sex households, specific subgroups at the intersections of race, gender, and household composition are falling through the cracks, particularly in self-reported mental health. The findings point to a mismatch between the credit's design, whose generosity scales with dependents, and the distinct structural factors that shape poverty for LGBTQ+ people.

Supported by the Williams Institute at UCLA School of Law.

Queer-ying State Legislatures: A Latent Variable Approach to Measuring Queer-Friendly State Legislative Districts

Kellen A. Kane

Who holds power in a legislature shapes which health policies are even considered possible. Using an original dataset of openly LGBTQ+ state legislators, I build a new measure of district queer-friendliness. Districts that elect LGBTQ+ legislators tend to be more liberal, more college-educated, higher-income, and less religious, and the measure identifies districts likely to elect an LGBTQ+ legislator that have not yet done so. Drawn from my dissertation, which received APSA's 2022 Kenneth Sherrill Prize, I am extending this measure through the most recent election cycles.
